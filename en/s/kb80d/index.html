Someone reported me, I was fired or expelled

The guides below are drafts: a lawyer has not reviewed them yet. This is not legal advice.

Right now

  1. Keep copies of orders, correspondence and the denunciation, but not on a work computer.
  2. Write any explanation briefly, without your views: it may reach the police.
  3. Don't sign a 'voluntary' resignation under pressure.

Draft What human rights defenders advise

Reported by colleagues or students, fired or expelled
  • A denunciation is not a case in itself. But after one you may be called in by the head, by the police or for an 'informal chat'. By law (art. 51 of the Constitution) you don't have to testify against yourself; in practice refusing to answer sometimes angers the officials, so decide with a lawyer how to handle it. A written explanation for your employer can end up with the police: keep it short and don't describe your views.
  • Dismissal. You cannot be fired for political views (art. 3 of the Labour Code), so employers look for other grounds: lateness, absence, or an 'immoral act' (art. 81(1)(8), only for people who teach or bring up children). People are often asked to resign 'voluntarily'. OVD-Info advises not to sign such a letter: pressure is hard to prove later. Before any penalty the employer must ask for a written explanation and give you 2 working days (art. 193).
  • Deadlines. A dismissal claim goes to court within one month of the day you were given a copy of the order or your employment record (art. 392). Don't miss it. Keep copies of every document and hand in letters in two copies, with a stamp showing receipt. Publicity sometimes helps people get reinstated and sometimes leads to a new denunciation, so decide this with a lawyer.
  • Students. The Education Act gives no direct ground for expelling someone for their views or for protests. The university must ask for a written explanation and give 3 study days for it, and can only penalise within a month of learning about the offence, taking the student council's view into account. Expulsion can be appealed to the university's disputes commission, to Rosobrnadzor and in court. According to OVD-Info and Groza, from 2022 to 2024 at least 70 students were expelled and 32 teachers were fired or forced out.
  • What may follow. A denunciation can turn into a charge of 'discrediting the army' (art. 20.3.3 of the Administrative Code), and a repeat within a year is already a criminal case (art. 280.3 of the Criminal Code). Where to turn: Net Freedoms Project and First Department; for teachers, the 'Uchitel' trade union. OVD-Info has detailed guides on dismissal and expulsion. OVD-Info is designated 'extremist' in Russia, so contact it from a device you will not have to hand over if detained. First Line offers psychological support.
  • A call 'about a report'. Usually this is a check (art. 144 of the Criminal Procedure Code): a decision is due in 3 days, extendable to 10 or 30. A call is no summons. OVD-Info ('extremist' in Russia) advises asking who is calling and why and requesting a written summons; at the door, ask for the KUSP number (the number under which police registered the report) and check it with the duty desk. A lawyer may attend.
  • Who reported you. You may not find out. If a charge sheet is drawn up, the report is usually in the file, which you may read (art. 25.1 of the Administrative Code). You can request check materials affecting your rights (art. 24 of the Constitution), but the reporter's identity may be concealed. Anonymous reports usually stay out of the file; secret informants' names are a state secret.
  • Responding to the reporter. A false denunciation (art. 306 of the Criminal Code) means knowingly lying about a crime. An accurate account of your words, or opinions like 'anti-Russian activity', are not covered. A defamation claim over a police report succeeds only if it had no basis and aimed to cause harm (Supreme Court Plenum, 2005). Defenders advise not contacting the reporter or arguing in public.
Safety
  • Now: keep copies of orders, correspondence with your employer and the denunciation itself if you were shown it, and store them somewhere other than a work computer.
  • Traces: your employer can see work email, school and work chats and corporate messengers, and colleagues or students forward screenshots of private chats.
  • Don't: discuss the war or politics in work chats or with students, describe your views in a written explanation, or sign a 'voluntary' resignation under pressure.
  • Now: write down who called or came, when, and from which unit, and the KUSP number if given; keep threats like 'I'll report you' and messages about any conflict with the person who may have reported you.
  • Traces: courts have already treated as public a conversation with an outsider present, a private video call and a message in a building chat; the other person may be recording.
  • Don't: do not write to the reporter or publish their details; after a summons, a charge sheet or a criminal case, do not delete anything without talking to a lawyer.

Designations of organisations in this guide: OVD-Info (foreign agent, extremist, on Rosfinmonitoring list). Contacting them carries a risk: do not write from a phone that may be seized, and do not repost their materials.

Independent trade union of education workers: legal advice for teachers, including dismissal for an 'immoral act' and pressure from school management.

First Line

Free, anonymous psychological support for people affected by, or afraid of, political persecution and for their families; an information line and safety guides.

Psychological help is in a separate bot, @PsyFirstlineBot: free, anonymous, one-off chat sessions with trained volunteer psychologists, 18+, no diagnoses or medication. Confidentiality is broken if there is a risk of suicide or a threat to a child.
